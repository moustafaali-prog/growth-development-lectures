3Growth Charts

Growth charts use a series of percentile curves to demonstrate the distribution of body measurements in children.

Growth Charts

Speaker notes Plot on Percentile Charts: You plot these measurements on percentile charts, which are graphs that display a range of values from the lowest (0th percentile) to the highest (100th percentile). Each percentile line represents a specific percentage of the population. For example, the 50th percentile represents the median or middle value for the general population. Comparison: By plotting a child's measurements on these charts, you can see where their measurements fall in relation to the general population. For instance, if a child's height measurement is on the 75th percentile line, it means that their height is greater than or equal to 75% of children in the reference population. Interpretation: The interpretation of these percentile measurements depends on the specific parameter being assessed. For example, if a child consistently falls below the 5th percentile for weight, it might indicate underweight or slow growth, while measurements consistently above the 95th percentile might indicate overweight or rapid growth. However, it's essential to consider various factors, including genetics and individual growth patterns, when interpreting percentile data.

6Slide 6

Growth Percentiles Establish a Child's Relative Position within Reference Norms

Understanding Growth Percentiles

Percentile Definition: A statistical value indicating the percentage of reference children who fall at or below a specific measurement.

50th Percentile: Represents the exact median parameter for age and sex. Half of healthy peers are larger, half are smaller.

Normal Range: Generally defined between the 5th and 95th percentiles on standardized WHO/CDC curves.

Trend vs. Static Value: A single percentile reading is merely a snapshot. Serial measurements over time (the growth curve) are clinically vital.

Clinical Interpretation Example

Key Rule from Wong's Textbook:

"Percentiles are not grades in school. Being in the 10th percentile is not 'worse' than being in the 90th percentile. Consistency along an established trajectory is the primary indicator of normal physical growth."

Case Scenario: 10th Percentile Height

If a 12-month-old boy is in the 10th percentile for length:• He is taller than 10% of 12-month-old boys.• 90% of peers are taller than him.• Clinical Assessment: HEALTHY if he has consistently tracked along the 10th curve since birth.

Steps 2–6: select, record, calculate, plot, interpret

Source: CDC (2013) Use and Interpretation of the WHO and CDC Growth Charts, Steps 2–6.

9

2 Select the chart

By age and sex; WHO <2 y, CDC ≥2 y.

3 Record data

Parents’ stature, gestational age and birth data (<2 y); date, age, weight and length/stature immediately after measuring; comments.

4 Calculate BMI (≥2 y)

BMI = kg ÷ cm ÷ cm × 10,000, one decimal place.

5 Plot

Age on the horizontal axis; draw a vertical line up. Measurement on the vertical axis; draw across. Dot at the intersection. (Weight-for-length: length on the horizontal axis.)

6 Interpret

Read the percentile; apply the cut-off for the index; compare with previous visits for major shifts.

Age rounding

<2 y: nearest month. 2–20 y: nearest ¼ year.

Speaker notes Age is determined to the nearest month under 2 years and to the nearest quarter-year from 2 to 20 years. BMI = weight (kg) ÷ stature (cm) ÷ stature (cm) × 10,000.

Interpreting the plotted point: cut-offs

Source: CDC (2013) guide, Step 6 table. Healthy-weight band (5th to <85th) is the CDC BMI-for-age category. Percentile ranks signal need for monitoring, not diagnosis.

11

Chart setIndexPercentileNutritional indicator
WHO (<2 y)Length-for-age< 2ndShort stature
WHO (<2 y)Weight-for-length< 2ndLow weight-for-length
WHO (<2 y)Weight-for-length> 98thHigh weight-for-length
CDC (2–19 y)BMI-for-age< 5thUnderweight
CDC (2–19 y)BMI-for-age5th to < 85thHealthy weight
CDC (2–19 y)BMI-for-age85th to < 95thOverweight
CDC (2–19 y)BMI-for-age≥ 95thObesity
CDC (2–19 y)Stature-for-age< 5thShort stature
Speaker notes If the percentile rank indicates a nutrition-related concern, additional monitoring and assessment are recommended (CDC). When transitioning from WHO to CDC charts at 2 years a change in classification may occur; interpret with caution. A 10-month-old’s length plots below the 2nd percentile on the WHO chart → short stature; assess further. A 14-month-old’s weight-for-length plots above the 98th → high weight-for-length. A 5-year-old’s BMI-for-age plots at the 90th percentile → overweight (between the 85th and 95th). An 8-year-old’s BMI-for-age plots at the 96th → obesity (at or above the 95th). A 4-year-old’s BMI-for-age plots at the 3rd → underweight (below the 5th).

Growth patterns that need attention

Sources: CDC (2013) guide; first three bullets carried over from the original course deck (textbook-based) — verify wording against the textbook page.

12

Height and weight percentiles widely disparate (for example, height near the 10th and weight near the 90th).

Failure to follow the expected growth velocity, especially during rapid growth in infancy and adolescence.

A sudden increase (except in puberty), decrease or no change in a previously steady pattern.

A major shift in percentile rank compared with previous visits (CDC).

A plotted point beyond the cut-offs for that index (previous slide).

Before escalating, check

Equipment calibration and technique · correct chart and sex · age (corrected for preterm) · units · parents’ stature, birth weight, gestational age · chronic illness or special health care needs.

Speaker notes Always recheck measurement technique, chart, sex and corrected age before escalating.

14Slide 14

Nursing procedure in health institutions (nursing process)

Measurement and plotting steps: CDC (2013). Nursing-process framing is general; adapt to the institution’s protocol and diagnosis taxonomy.

14

PhaseNursing actions
AssessmentPrepare and check equipment; identify the child; explain to the caregiver; measure; collect gestational age, birth weight, feeding history, parents’ stature, illness; select chart; plot; compare with previous plots.
DiagnosisIdentify actual or risk problems related to growth and nutrition using the institution’s taxonomy.
PlanningSet measurable goals with the family; set the follow-up measurement schedule.
ImplementationFeeding and breastfeeding support; nutrition counselling; parent education; report or refer to the physician or dietitian when a cut-off or pattern concern is present; document.
EvaluationRe-measure at follow-up; compare the new plot with earlier points; revise the plan.
