ROTATION: KINDERGARTEN

Chapter 6 — Kindergarten Rotation

Health assessment and screening of the preschool child (3-6 years): site role and readiness

concepts, the preschool history and physical, vision and hearing screening with referral criteria,

developmental and school-readiness screening, play and socialization, common problems, and

health-education activities.

Age range: 3 – 6 years

6.1 Site Profile — Role of the Kindergarten

Preschool should be used primarily as an opportunity to foster the child's social skills and

accustom the child to the group environment. Selecting a preschool considers accreditation,

teachers' qualifications, other parents' recommendations, and the structure of the daily schedule.

What the nurse brings: the preschooler "shows off" dramatically and enjoys imaginative, dramatic

play — assessment and teaching are built around play.

Readiness principle: there are no absolute indicators for school readiness; the child's social

maturation, especially attention span, is as important as academic readiness.

Readiness is influenced by: social, emotional, and physical development; health status; ability and

desire to learn; life experiences; family environment; and parental support.

Risk factors to screen for: insecure attachment in the early years, maternal depression, parental

substance abuse, and low socioeconomic status — screen and refer when present.

By kindergarten entry the child should have developed cooperation, sharing (of things and

feelings), kindness, generosity, affection display, conversation, expression of feelings, helping others,

and making friends.

6.2 Preschool Health Assessment

The well-child clinic model (Ch. 4) applies: history, measurements, screening, then guidance. The

preschooler can now cooperate with instructions, so the examination is more efficient.

6.3 Vision & Hearing Screening

VISION

Indication: the USPSTF recommends vision screening for the presence of amblyopia and its risk

factors for all children 3-5 years.

Alignment tests: corneal light reflex (Hirschberg) from about 40.5 cm (16 in) — light symmetrical in

normal eyes, off-center in misalignment; epicanthal folds can mimic misalignment

(pseudostrabismus, common in Asian children). Cover test at near (33 cm / 13 in) and far (6 m / 20

ft); alternate cover test.

Strabismus window: if misalignment is not detected and corrected by ages 4-6 years, blindness

from disuse (amblyopia) may result.

Acuity: the tumbling E and HOTV are excellent tests for preschool-age children; other options:

Snellen letters/numbers, picture test, Allen figures, LEA symbols.

Referral criterion: fewer than 4 of 6 correct on the 20-foot (6-m) line.

Peripheral vision (cooperative child): normal ~50° upward, 70° downward, 60° nasalward, 90°

temporally.

HEARING

Recommendation: the AAP recommends pure-tone audiometry screening at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz;

a child fails if the tones cannot be heard at 20 dB HL.

Age-appropriate methods: behavioral audiometry for toddlers; play audiometry for the toddler;

pure-tone audiometry for children and adolescents; tympanometry (middle-ear function) at all ages;

EOAE for cochlear (outer hair cell) response.

Suspicion: maintain a high index of suspicion for children with conditions associated with hearing

loss, whose parents report concern, or whose behavior suggests auditory impairment.

Figure 6.1 shows the three referral triggers of the preschool screening program: an acuity below

the 20-foot-line standard, any constant misalignment (especially within the amblyopia window

before ages 4-6), or failure of the pure-tone hearing screen.

SPEECH REFERRAL NOTE

Stuttering with no improvement over more than 6 months, or speech unintelligible to outside

listeners beyond the expected age, prompts evaluation (see the red-flag principle in Ch. 4 §4.6).

Referral thresholds for the district screening policy follow the official screening program.

6.5 Play, Behavior & Socialization

Associative play: preschoolers especially enjoy associative play — group play in similar or identical

activities; the parallel play of the toddler (alongside, not with others) gives way to group activity.

Imagination: activity is imitative, imaginative, and dramatic; dress-up is central; imaginary friends

appear 2½-3 years and last until school entry; most fears resolve by 5-6 years.

6Page 6

Sharing & friendships: by kindergarten entry the child cooperates, shares (things and feelings),

shows kindness/generosity/affection, holds conversation, expresses feelings, helps others, and makes

friends.

Separation: during the preschool period the separation-individuation phase matures; preschoolers

tolerate brief separations with little or no protest — the nurse normalizes this for worried parents.

Discipline: the preschooler responds to consistent limit-setting with simple, concrete explanations —

words are taken literally (magical thinking), so the parent says "that was a bad thing to do," not "you

are bad".

6.6 Common Problems in the Kindergarten

Problem

Guidance

Infections

Children commonly develop infections when entering day care or preschool because of

new exposure; symptom/exclusion policy per the facility (see Ch. 5)

Dental caries

Caries are present in ~60% of children by age 5; prevention = daily brushing and flossing,

pea-sized fluoride toothpaste without excess (fluorosis), parent performs flossing, avoid

cariogenic foods — rinse with water after sugary foods

Accidental

ingestion &

injury

Accidental injury is the leading cause of death in children 1-14 years; preschoolers can

absorb concrete safety teaching but may not transfer it — close supervision continues;

accidental ingestion risk is high — hazardous substances stored out of reach, never coax

swallowing

Nutrition

Preschool nutrition values in Ch. 4 §4.4 (≈90 kcal/kg ≈ 1800 cal/day; fluids ~100

ml/kg/day; protein 13-19 g/day; fiber = age + 5; saturated fat <10%; calcium 700 mg 1-3

yr / 1000 mg 4-8 yr; juice cap 4-6 oz); obesity in young children has increased

dramatically

Screening

findings

Refer positive vision/hearing (6.3) and developmental findings (6.4); document and

communicate with the family (Ch. 9)

6.7 Health Education Activities

Hand hygiene: standard infection-control hand washing before and after every meal, diapering, and

child contact.

7Page 7

Dental hygiene program: demonstrate pea-sized toothpaste brushing; parents (not the child) floss;

schedule dental examinations per policy.

Safety lessons: an adult accompanies at street crossings; a bicycle helmet is worn on any ride; the

child never approaches pools or bodies of water alone; medications and cleaning products are never

touched.

Literacy: daily reading/storytelling with question-and-answer supports language and school

readiness.

Group readiness practice: turn-taking, sharing, following three-part commands (a skill expected at 4

years).
