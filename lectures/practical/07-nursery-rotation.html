ROTATION: NURSERY DAYCARE

Chapter 5 — Nursery Rotation

Daily care and health supervision of healthy children 0-3 years in a daycare/nursery setting: intake

assessment, morning health checks, feeding and hygiene procedures, growth monitoring with

developmental observation, common problems, and medication/emergency readiness.

Age range: Birth – 3 years

5.1 Site Profile — Role of the Nursery

Many mothers work outside the home, there are many single-parent families, and many families

live far from relatives — in all of these circumstances infants may need to be cared for outside

the home, in child care settings or home day care centers. The nursery rotation is where the

nurse observes and supports that daily care.

Types: sitter in the home, traditional (freestanding) day care center, or home-based day care center

with fewer children.

Licensing: families using a freestanding or home-based center should make sure the provider is

appropriately licensed.

Caregiver-to-child ratio: parents should feel comfortable with the ratio; the number of caregivers

per child follows the licensed facility's staffing plan.

Staff training: caregivers should be trained in infant CPR and first aid — and parents should be

taught first-aid measures and infant/child CPR as well, to minimize the consequences of injury.

Separation anxiety: it is helpful to visit the center once or twice beforehand so the infant gets used

to the caregivers from the comfort of the parent's lap. Warn parents that separation anxiety in late

infancy can cause a disturbing crying episode when the parent leaves — and reassure them the infant

will not suffer harm from the separation.

Admission to the nursery starts with a health history and immunization verification, a baseline

physical assessment, and a written emergency plan. The same periodic-screening principle

applies in the daycare as in the clinic: periodic screening for adequate growth and development,

adherence to the recommended screenings, and the current immunization schedule.

RULE OF THUMB FOR RASHES AND FEVER

A fever (temperature above the normal range) together with a new generalised rash, lethargy, or

poor feeding is a call-the-provider situation. Teething does not cause high fever, vomiting, or

diarrhea — a temperature elevation with those signs means illness, not teeth. Exclusion criteria (for

example "keep home until fever-free for 24 hours") follow each licensed facility's written policy.

5.4 Daily Care Procedures

Feeding by stage (0-3 years)

Infant fluid & calories: fluid 140-160 mL/kg/day (newborn); ~100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg plus

50 mL/kg/day for the next 10 kg; calories 105-108 kcal/kg/day (1-6 months: 108 kcal/kg; 6-12

months: 98 kcal/kg).

Breastfeeding at the center: expressed breast milk fed by bottle — never microwaved; feed on the

baby's cues; the properly latched infant sucks rhythmically with audible swallowing.

Formula: prepare exactly per package instructions; 2-3 oz per feeding about 6×/day as a starter

guide; never prop the bottle and never put juice or sweetened liquid in a bottle.

Solids: begin with a single-ingredient iron-fortified cereal, then fruits and vegetables; introduce one

new food at a time and watch for reactions (Ch. 4 §4.4 infant).

Toddler meals: 3 meals + 2 snacks, small portions; milk 2-3 servings (24-28 oz/day equivalent); juice

capped at 4-6 oz/day of 100% juice in a cup.

Choking prevention while feeding: slowly add more difficult-to-chew foods as the toddler becomes

adept; cut all foods into bite-size pieces; avoid nuts, gumdrops/chewy candies, raw carrots, peanut

butter alone, and popcorn; cut hot dogs and grapes into quarters; cook carrots until soft or grate

them; always supervise the toddler while eating.

Self-feeding: child-sized spoon and fork with dull tines; seat the toddler securely with feet

supported, not dangling; never leave the toddler unattended while eating; minimize distractions (turn

off the TV).

Hygiene, bathing & diapering

Bathing safety: infants can drown in a very small amount of water — never leave an infant

unattended in a sink, baby bathtub, standard bathtub, swimming or wading pool, or any body of

water even if shallow; keep the bathroom door closed and the toilet lid down; empty tubs, pails, and

buckets immediately after use.

5Page 5

Diapering: changing diapers is part of the infant's daily care; the wet-diaper count (about six per 24

hours) is the caregiver's daily hydration check. Follow the center's universal-hygiene procedure at

every change (gloves, cleaning, hand hygiene, safe disposal).

Hand hygiene: handwashing is part of infant-care practice; apply standard infection-control hand

hygiene before and after every child contact, meal, and diaper change.

Safe sleep & teething

Safe sleep: place infants on the back for sleep (SIDS risk reduction); cribs must not have pillows,

comforters, stuffed animals, or other soft items; keep plastic bags of any size away from infants; keep

window-blind and drapery cords out of the infant's reach.

Teething: first lower central incisors appear ~6-10 months (average 8 months); cold items are

soothing; fever, vomiting, or diarrhea are not teething signs — treat them as illness.

Play & stimulation

Infants: toys recommended for 0-12 months only; avoid stuffed animals with eyes or buttons that

can be dislodged; keep the floor free of small dropped items.

Toddlers: parallel play (alongside, not with, others) with imitation — the best "toy" is adult-child

interaction.

Reading, storytelling, and interactive adult-child communication positively affect language

development.

Growth and development are monitored at every contact. The nurse weighs and measures on a

routine schedule, plots on the correct chart, and observes milestones during daily care.

5.7 Medication & Emergencies

SAFE MEDICATION HANDLING IN THE NURSERY

All medications have child-safety caps; never leave any medication within the toddler's reach.

Medications, household cleaning supplies, and other hazardous substances are stored completely

out of reach.

Be especially careful with medications provided in child-resistant containers — "child-resistant" is

not "child-proof".

Administration, dose check, documentation, and parent permission follow the licensed center's

written policy.

First-aid & CPR readiness: providers/caregivers should be trained in infant CPR and first aid, and

parents should be taught first-aid measures and infant/child CPR to minimize the consequences of

injury.

Emergency action plan: a written plan covering choking, injury, allergic reaction, and fever; staff

roles, call sequence, who documents, and parent notification. Call-for-help numbers follow the

facility's emergency plan.

Documentation: incident reports, medication records, and daily observation logs (report templates

in Ch. 9).
