ROTATION: WELL-CHILD CLINIC

Chapter 4 — Well-Child Clinic

The central practical site for health supervision: the recommended visit schedule, what happens at

each visit, age-specific anticipatory guidance for every stage (0-20 yr), immunization administration

in the clinic, and a milestone quick reference with red flags.

Age range: Birth – 20 years

4.1 Site Profile — Role of the Well-Child Clinic

The well-child clinic is where positive health is promoted in normal, healthy children. Visits focus

primarily on anticipatory guidance — teaching what to expect next and how to prevent harm

before the child reaches the susceptible age — ideally beginning during pregnancy.

Goal: periodic screening and the prevention of disease. The AAP recommends periodic check-ups for

all infants and children.

Nurse's role: carry out the recommended periodic screenings and the current immunization

schedule; educate the family at every visit.

Anticipatory guidance: one third of all childhood injuries occur in the home — most are predictable

and preventable when teaching precedes the susceptible age.

Cultural respect: explore the family's cultural practices around growth and development; most are

harmless and can be supported. Never assume practices from skin color, accent, or name.

FOUR SKILLS USED AT EVERY VISIT

History: interval history — feeding, elimination, sleep, behavior, past illnesses, allergies,

medications, immunization record.

Measurements & plotting: length/height, weight, head circumference, weight-for-length or BMI,

and increasingly blood pressure.

Screening: vision, hearing, and developmental/behavioral screening and surveillance.

Guidance & procedures: physical examination, immunizations and other procedures, then

anticipatory guidance and parent education.

Measurement & plotting rules

Length: supine on a length board until 24 months (or 36 months via the birth-36-month chart); a

tape measure or wall chart is never used. Measure at least twice (ideally 3 times) with values agreeing

within 0.5 cm; use the mean.

Height: standing with a stadiometer; wall charts and flip-up bars are not used.

Weight: nude for the birth-36-month charts; balance-beam scale to the nearest 10 g (infant) or 100 g

(child).

Head circumference: measured and plotted through 36 months; head and chest circumferences are

roughly equal at about 1-2 years.

Charts: WHO growth standards 0-2 years (breastfed reference); CDC charts for 2-20 years with BMI-

for-age.

Interpretation: a normal, well-nourished child plots between the 5th and 95th percentiles; below

the 5th or above the 95th suggests deficiency or excess. A child at the 5th percentile can be as

normal as one above the 95th — the most reliable evaluation is the trend over time.

Concern: widely disparate percentiles (e.g., height 10th + weight 90th), failure to follow expected

growth velocity, or a sudden change in a previously steady pattern.

Technique: when growth is a concern, take serial measurements at the same time of day (afternoon

height is less than morning height).

Figure 4.3 shows the percentile bands on a growth chart: adequate nutrition falls between the

5th and 95th percentiles; for BMI-for-age the 85th-95th band marks risk and the 95th marks

overweight. These rules are given in the measurement tips above.

4.4 Age-Specific Health Supervision

Anticipatory guidance is matched to each developmental stage. The five blocks below give the

essential teaching points for each age.

Feeding topic

Honey in the first 12 months (infant botulism); propping a bottle or bottle-in-bed; juice in

Cup

milestones

Lips to cup rim 4-5 months; drinks from a cup with help 7-9 months; drinks from a cup (with

spilling) 9-12 months

Weaning

Gradual, one feeding at a time; the nighttime feeding is discontinued last; readiness signals

appear in the second half of the first year

Sleep: place the infant to sleep on the back at all times; firm mattress, no pillows or loose blankets;

crib slats spacing ≤6 cm; no drop-side cribs. Most infants sleep 9-11 hours nightly at 3-4 months

(~15 hr/day total); ~11.7 hr nightly by 1 year.

Pacifier: AAP supports limited use in the first 6 months (analgesia + SIDS protection), weaning in the

second 6 months; one-piece construction with a loop handle; never on a string around the neck.

Car seats: all infants and toddlers ride rear-facing for as long as possible — until they reach the

highest weight or height allowed by the car-seat manufacturer (most convertible seats allow

rear-facing past age 2; older editions: "until 12 months AND 20 lb" — report the current

recommendation); rear-facing seat never in a front seat with a passenger air bag; back seat is safest;

~52% of newborn seats are misused at hospital discharge.

Dental: first teeth (lower central incisors) 6-10 months, average 8 months; teeth ≈ age in months

minus 6; clean gums/teeth with a damp cloth; fluoridated toothpaste "smear" for children <2 years;

oral examination by 6 months and a dental home by 1 year; fluoride supplement from 6 months if

the water is inadequate; watch the infant for earaches/circumoral cyanosis.

Teething: fever >39°C, vomiting, or diarrhea means illness — not teething; cold soothes; no teething

powders, aspirin, or liquor on the gums; no amber necklaces.

Ears & language: cooing / simple vowel sounds and laughing aloud appear about 4-5 months;

babbling with inflection 7-10 months. Warning signs: no sounds or babbling by 4 months; not

laughing or squealing by 6 months.

Play: solitary; suggested toys 0-6 months (mobiles, unbreakable mirror, music box, rattles) and 6-12

months (blocks, nested cups, picture books, ball, cup and spoon, wood puzzles, push-pull toys);

restrict TV and recorded video in children <2 years.

8Page 8

Discipline: infants cannot grasp cause-and-effect of danger — childproof the environment; begin

with a negative voice and stern eye contact, then time-out (play yard preferred over a chair); there is

no evidence that meeting needs spoils a child.

INFANT RED FLAGS

No smile at people at 3 months; refuses cuddling; no interest in peek-a-boo at 8 months (social-

emotional)

No head support 3-4 months; no sitting with assistance by 6 months; no crawling by 12 months; no

supported standing by 12 months

Not pulling to stand by 11-12 months → evaluate for developmental hip dysplasia

Strabismus / lack of binocularity can cause permanent blindness if undetected

Toddler — 1-3 years

Nutrition: 1000-1400 kcal/day (moderately active boy, 2-3 yr, 2010 Dietary Guidelines); growth slows

12-18 months and physiologic anorexia (decreased appetite) appears around 18 months — a normal,

not alarming, event; 3 meals + 2 snacks with small portions; milk 2-3 servings (24-28 oz/day — more

than a quart limits solids and leads to iron/nutrient deficiency); low-fat (2%) milk after age 2; juice

capped at 4-6 oz/day of 100% juice.

Weaning: drink from a cup at the first birthday; weaning from the bottle is complete by 15 months

(the 12-15-month window); never a bedtime bottle — ends "nursing bottle caries" (early childhood

caries); dental care: water-only brushing before age 2, then a pea-sized amount of fluoridated

toothpaste beginning at 2 years.

Nutrition numbers: iron EAR 7 mg/day (1-3 yr); calcium AI 500 mg/day (1-3 yr).

Negativism: it is a necessary assertion of control, not stubbornness — reduce the opportunities for

"no" (offer two acceptable choices); if refused, the parent calmly decides; ritualism (need for

sameness) is comforting and helps through hospitalization.

Tantrums: common when ill, hungry, frustrated, tired (worst near bedtime); three quarters last ≤5

minutes; manage by ignoring the behavior unless injurious, staying present, comforting once it

subsides, and praising non-tantrum behavior. Red flags: tantrums past 5 years, lasting over 15

minutes, or more than 5 times a day → possible serious problem. Time-outs work well from ~18

months.

Toilet training: readiness has five markers (bladder, bowel, cognitive, motor, psychologic) —

sphincter control usually by 22-30 months, dry ≥2 hours, waking from the nap dry, regular bowel

movements, sits/walks/squats, removes clothing; mental readiness (recognizes the urge,

9Page 9

communicates, imitates); psychologic (wishes to please, sits 5-8 min); and parental readiness (no

family stress from move, divorce, new sibling). Average initiation ~20.6 months; girls 2-2.5 months

before boys; no benefit of intensive training before 27 months; bowel training precedes bladder;

nighttime dryness takes months-years (bedwetting normal in girls ≤4, boys ≤5; no night dryness by 6

years → intervene). Regression is a normal temporary setback.

Sleep: ~11-12 hours with one nap; bedtime resistance and fear of monsters are common later —

regular routine, transitional object, snack, TV out of the room.

Media: television before age 2 decreases language skills; total quality media ≤1 hour/day for

toddlers.

Play: parallel play (alongside, not with others) with imitation; the best "toy" is adult-child interaction.

Safety: unintentional injury is the leading cause of death ages 1-19 (~37%); rear-facing car seat for as

long as possible up to the manufacturer's weight/height limit, then a forward-facing seat with

harness, then a belt-positioning booster until 145 cm (4 ft 9 in) or 8-12 years; gates at both stair ends;

waterproofing — the highest drowning rate occurs in 1- to 2-year-olds, so constant supervision is

needed in and around all water; water heater ≤49°C; poisoning only locked cabinets protect

(cosmetics, analgesics, cleaners top exposures); hot-car hyperthermia deaths are preventable.

Preschooler — 3-6 years

Development: Erikson initiative vs guilt; the superego/conscience develops; magical thinking makes

words literal — say "that was a bad thing to do," not "you are bad"; identification with the same-sex

parent resolves the Oedipal stage.

Fears: the greatest number and variety of fears appear in the preschool years (dark, being left alone,

animals, ghosts, annihilation); most resolve by 5-6 years. Management: night-light,

desensitization/modeling, relaxation, brave self-talk. Refer severe fears that disrupt family life.

Imaginary friends usually appear 2½-3 years and last until school entry (more common in firstborns

and girls) — a sign of health; parents may acknowledge the friend but the child stays responsible for

behavior.

Play: associative play (similar activity, no rigid rules); dramatic/imitative play is central; 4-5-year-olds

reproduce significant-adult behavior.

Language: vocabulary grows from ~300 words at 2 years to >2100 by the end of 5 years.

School readiness: no single readiness indicator; social maturation (especially attention span) matters

as much as academics; school begins between ages 5 and 6.

Nutrition: ~90 kcal/kg ≈ 1800 kcal/day; fluids ~100 ml/kg/day; protein 13-19 g/day; fiber = age + 5

g; saturated fat <10% of calories; calcium 1000 mg/day (4-8 yr); juice/sweetened drinks ≤4-6 oz/day;

10Page 10

follow the 5-2-1-0 framework (5 fruits/vegetables, ≤2 hours of screen, 0 sugar-sweetened drinks, 1

hour activity); preschoolers eat about half an adult portion.

Sleep & dental: ~12 hours nightly with infrequent naps; night waking is common. Primary dentition

is complete by the start of the preschool years; brushing still needs adult assistance and flossing is a

parent's task; continue professional care and fluoride.

Discipline: verbal limit-setting works better than with toddlers — the child obeys danger warnings;

allowing questions and disagreement fosters independence; time-out and solitary play stop

aggression; spanking teaches aggression "as a lesson"; encourage verbal expression of feelings.

Safety: less prone to falls and more aware of danger; poisoning still dangerous (mouthing has

ceased); pedestrian-vehicle injuries increase; teaching replaces pure protection — parents model

behavior (bicycle helmets); electrical-outlet caps can be discontinued with an explanation.

Red flags: severe fears disrupting family life; extreme aggression (needs professional treatment);

stuttering with no improvement over >6 months → speech evaluation; injury-prevention education

for 4½-year-old risk takers.

School-age — 6-12 years

Growth: ~5 cm/yr; weight gain 2-3 kg/yr; average 6-year-old ≈116 cm/21 kg; average 12-year-old

≈150 cm/40 kg; girls surpass boys in height and weight near the end. Prepubescence is the ~2-year

period preceding puberty; first physiologic signs about 9 years (girls). All primary teeth are shed in

this span — the first tooth (central mandibular incisors) is lost at about 6 years ("age of the loose

tooth"); the 6-year molar erupts behind the deciduous molars; 60% of children have dental caries by

age 5.

Psychosocial: Erikson industry vs inferiority — competence through skills; peers become central

(same-sex "best friends", clubs with codes and conformity); bullying affects ~25% of elementary

students as bully or victim, and cyberbullying can be more harmful (anonymous, wide audience);

teach reporting and family conversation. Physical maturity ≠ emotional maturity.

Cognitive: concrete operations 7-11 years; conservation of number 5-6, liquid/mass/length 6-7,

weight 9-10, volume 9-12; reading is the most significant school skill; homework: quiet, well-lit area,

no TV until done, explain rather than answer.

Nutrition: caloric needs are lower than preschool and lower than the adolescent growth spurt;

children eat what the family eats; encourage fruit, nuts, and wholesome snacks; obesity prevalence is

stable at ~17% and higher in rural/socioeconomically distressed families; school-lunch programs

must supply 1/3 of the daily RDA.

Safety: motor-vehicle crashes are the most common cause of severe accidental injury/death (as

passenger or pedestrian); children <13 years ride in the rear, never the front passenger seat with an

11Page 11

air bag; over 18 kg (40 lb) use a forward-facing convertible seat if it fits, then a booster until the lap-

shoulder belt fits; no one rides in a pickup-truck bed; ATVs are not recommended under 16 years;

bicycle helmets cut head injury ~45%, brain injury ~33%, facial injury ~27%, fatal injury ~29% —

helmet every ride, the bike must fit; trampolines: single jumper, no somersaults, ≥6 years; skateboards

<5 years never, 6-10 only with close supervision and full padding; participation physical exams ≥every

2 years for sports; firearms locked.

Sleep & dental: sleep falls from ~11.5 hours at 5 years to ~9 hours at 11; up to 50% of children have

nightmares; sleepwalking is common (3-5% frequent) in the first 3-4 hours of sleep. Children may

need help with brushing until ~7-10 years — parents monitor and floss at least daily; brush 2-3×/day

for 2-3 minutes with fluoridated toothpaste; dental examinations every 6 months; dental sealants are

recommended for all school-age children.

Discipline: no corporal punishment — reasoning is effective; time-out for acute behavior; advanced

strategies include withholding privileges, recompense, penalties, and contracting; problem-solving is

the best limit-setting approach; discipline works best in a positive, supportive relationship.

Guidance box highlights: age 6 — ravenous appetite, bicycle safety, privacy (separate bedroom),

peer interaction; ages 7-10 — allergies may appear, minor injuries increase, growth of peer

involvement, independence with limits; ages 11-12 — prepare for pubescent body changes, accurate

sex education, allow regression while supporting growing up.

Stress signals (explore further): stomach pains/headache, sleep problems, bed-wetting, eating-

habit changes, aggressive/stubborn behavior, reluctance to participate, regression (e.g., thumb

sucking).

Adolescent — 12-20 years

The full adolescent assessment (confidentiality, Tanner staging, HEEADSSS interview, risk-behavior

screening, annual visits) is built in Chapter 8. Guidance points that belong to every clinic visit here:

Annual influenza vaccination (live-attenuated or inactivated) is recommended for all adolescents.

Tdap: recommended at 11-18 years for those who completed the childhood DTaP/DTP series and

have not received a tetanus booster; may be given before the 5-year Td interval to close the pertussis

gap.

Meningococcal (MenACWY): at 11-12 years with a booster before age 16; a single dose at 13-18

years if never vaccinated.

HPV: the 9-valent vaccine (Gardasil 9) from a minimum age of 9 years, routine at 11-12 years; two

doses if the series starts at 9-14 years, three doses for a start at 15 years or later or in

immunocompromised persons.

12Page 12

Varicella: for those without a history of infection — two doses ≥4 weeks apart for ages ≥13 years.

Hepatitis B: three-dose series for anyone not previously covered. Hepatitis A in targeted areas or

when immunity is desired.

Confidentiality: offers of confidential services are critical for sensitive topics (sexual behavior,

substance use); many teenagers avoid care if they believe their parents will be told.

HEEADSSS interview (Ch. 8): Home / Education / Eating / Activities / Drugs / Sexuality / Suicide-

depression / Safety.

4.1 Site Profile — Role of the Well-Child Clinic

The well-child clinic is where positive health is promoted in normal, healthy children. Visits focus

primarily on anticipatory guidance — teaching what to expect next and how to prevent harm

before the child reaches the susceptible age — ideally beginning during pregnancy.

Goal: periodic screening and the prevention of disease. The AAP recommends periodic check-ups for

all infants and children.

Nurse's role: carry out the recommended periodic screenings and the current immunization

schedule; educate the family at every visit.

Anticipatory guidance: one third of all childhood injuries occur in the home — most are predictable

and preventable when teaching precedes the susceptible age.

Cultural respect: explore the family's cultural practices around growth and development; most are

harmless and can be supported. Never assume practices from skin color, accent, or name.

FOUR SKILLS USED AT EVERY VISIT

History: interval history — feeding, elimination, sleep, behavior, past illnesses, allergies,

medications, immunization record.

Measurements & plotting: length/height, weight, head circumference, weight-for-length or BMI,

and increasingly blood pressure.

Screening: vision, hearing, and developmental/behavioral screening and surveillance.

Guidance & procedures: physical examination, immunizations and other procedures, then

anticipatory guidance and parent education.

developmental screening, the physical examination, then procedures and parental guidance. The

workflow below is the same for every age; only the content changes. Figure 4.2 charts this

Measurement & plotting rules

Length: supine on a length board until 24 months (or 36 months via the birth-36-month chart); a

tape measure or wall chart is never used. Measure at least twice (ideally 3 times) with values agreeing

within 0.5 cm; use the mean.

Height: standing with a stadiometer; wall charts and flip-up bars are not used.

Weight: nude for the birth-36-month charts; balance-beam scale to the nearest 10 g (infant) or 100 g

(child).

Head circumference: measured and plotted through 36 months; head and chest circumferences are

roughly equal at about 1-2 years.

Charts: WHO growth standards 0-2 years (breastfed reference); CDC charts for 2-20 years with BMI-

for-age.

Interpretation: a normal, well-nourished child plots between the 5th and 95th percentiles; below

the 5th or above the 95th suggests deficiency or excess. A child at the 5th percentile can be as

normal as one above the 95th — the most reliable evaluation is the trend over time.

Concern: widely disparate percentiles (e.g., height 10th + weight 90th), failure to follow expected

growth velocity, or a sudden change in a previously steady pattern.

Technique: when growth is a concern, take serial measurements at the same time of day (afternoon

height is less than morning height).

Figure 4.3 shows the percentile bands on a growth chart: adequate nutrition falls between the

5th and 95th percentiles; for BMI-for-age the 85th-95th band marks risk and the 95th marks

overweight. These rules are given in the measurement tips above.

4.4 Age-Specific Health Supervision

Anticipatory guidance is matched to each developmental stage. The five blocks below give the

essential teaching points for each age.

Infant — 0-12 months

Feeding topic

Guidance

Solids

Do not add solids before 4-6 months (earlier: sixfold increase in obesity odds in formula-

fed infants); transition period ≈ 4-6 months. Iron-fortified infant cereal first (rice — easily

digested, low allergic potential); one new food at a time, every 5-7 days (one source says 4-

7, another 3-5 days); raw seeds/nuts/vegetables and grapes are aspiration hazards; cracked

zwieback ~6 months; junior/finger foods 8-9 months; well-cooked table foods at 1 year

Milk

Milk remains the main calorie source until after 12 months; whole cow's milk not before 12

months (iron/zinc/vitamin C deficient, high renal solute load); skim/low-fat also

unacceptable

Page 7 — Avoid

Avoid

Honey in the first 12 months (infant botulism); propping a bottle or bottle-in-bed; juice in

bottles, especially <6 months (enamel erosion, early childhood caries)

Supplements

Vitamin D 400 IU daily for all infants (those on <1 L/day of fortified formula included); iron

1 mg/kg/day if exclusively breastfed >4 months until iron-fortified foods start

Juice

Not required under 6 months and of no demonstrable benefit; only 100% juice; max 4-6

oz/day (1-6 yr); avoid apple, pear, prune, sweet cherry, peach, grape (fructose/sorbitol →

cramps/diarrhea); give sugared liquids in a cup

Cup

milestones

Lips to cup rim 4-5 months; drinks from a cup with help 7-9 months; drinks from a cup (with

spilling) 9-12 months

Weaning

Gradual, one feeding at a time; the nighttime feeding is discontinued last; readiness signals

appear in the second half of the first year

Sleep: place the infant to sleep on the back at all times; firm mattress, no pillows or loose blankets;

crib slats spacing ≤6 cm; no drop-side cribs. Most infants sleep 9-11 hours nightly at 3-4 months

(~15 hr/day total); ~11.7 hr nightly by 1 year.

Pacifier: AAP supports limited use in the first 6 months (analgesia + SIDS protection), weaning in the

second 6 months; one-piece construction with a loop handle; never on a string around the neck.

Car seats: all infants and toddlers ride rear-facing for as long as possible — until they reach the

highest weight or height allowed by the car-seat manufacturer (most convertible seats allow

rear-facing past age 2; older editions: "until 12 months AND 20 lb" — report the current

recommendation); rear-facing seat never in a front seat with a passenger air bag; back seat is safest;

~52% of newborn seats are misused at hospital discharge.

Dental: first teeth (lower central incisors) 6-10 months, average 8 months; teeth ≈ age in months

minus 6; clean gums/teeth with a damp cloth; fluoridated toothpaste "smear" for children <2 years;

oral examination by 6 months and a dental home by 1 year; fluoride supplement from 6 months if

the water is inadequate; watch the infant for earaches/circumoral cyanosis.

Teething: fever >39°C, vomiting, or diarrhea means illness — not teething; cold soothes; no teething

powders, aspirin, or liquor on the gums; no amber necklaces.

Ears & language: cooing / simple vowel sounds and laughing aloud appear about 4-5 months;

babbling with inflection 7-10 months. Warning signs: no sounds or babbling by 4 months; not

laughing or squealing by 6 months.

Play: solitary; suggested toys 0-6 months (mobiles, unbreakable mirror, music box, rattles) and 6-12

months (blocks, nested cups, picture books, ball, cup and spoon, wood puzzles, push-pull toys);

restrict TV and recorded video in children <2 years.

Toddler — 1-3 years

Nutrition: 1000-1400 kcal/day (moderately active boy, 2-3 yr, 2010 Dietary Guidelines); growth slows

12-18 months and physiologic anorexia (decreased appetite) appears around 18 months — a normal,

not alarming, event; 3 meals + 2 snacks with small portions; milk 2-3 servings (24-28 oz/day — more

than a quart limits solids and leads to iron/nutrient deficiency); low-fat (2%) milk after age 2; juice

capped at 4-6 oz/day of 100% juice.

Weaning: drink from a cup at the first birthday; weaning from the bottle is complete by 15 months

(the 12-15-month window); never a bedtime bottle — ends "nursing bottle caries" (early childhood

caries); dental care: water-only brushing before age 2, then a pea-sized amount of fluoridated

toothpaste beginning at 2 years.

Nutrition numbers: iron EAR 7 mg/day (1-3 yr); calcium AI 500 mg/day (1-3 yr).

Negativism: it is a necessary assertion of control, not stubbornness — reduce the opportunities for

"no" (offer two acceptable choices); if refused, the parent calmly decides; ritualism (need for

sameness) is comforting and helps through hospitalization.

Tantrums: common when ill, hungry, frustrated, tired (worst near bedtime); three quarters last ≤5

minutes; manage by ignoring the behavior unless injurious, staying present, comforting once it

subsides, and praising non-tantrum behavior. Red flags: tantrums past 5 years, lasting over 15

minutes, or more than 5 times a day → possible serious problem. Time-outs work well from ~18

months.

Toilet training: readiness has five markers (bladder, bowel, cognitive, motor, psychologic) —

sphincter control usually by 22-30 months, dry ≥2 hours, waking from the nap dry, regular bowel

movements, sits/walks/squats, removes clothing; mental readiness (recognizes the urge,

Page 9 — communicates, imitates); psychologic (wishes to please, sits 5-8 min); and parental readin

communicates, imitates); psychologic (wishes to please, sits 5-8 min); and parental readiness (no

family stress from move, divorce, new sibling). Average initiation ~20.6 months; girls 2-2.5 months

before boys; no benefit of intensive training before 27 months; bowel training precedes bladder;

nighttime dryness takes months-years (bedwetting normal in girls ≤4, boys ≤5; no night dryness by 6

years → intervene). Regression is a normal temporary setback.

Sleep: ~11-12 hours with one nap; bedtime resistance and fear of monsters are common later —

regular routine, transitional object, snack, TV out of the room.

Media: television before age 2 decreases language skills; total quality media ≤1 hour/day for

toddlers.

Play: parallel play (alongside, not with others) with imitation; the best "toy" is adult-child interaction.

Safety: unintentional injury is the leading cause of death ages 1-19 (~37%); rear-facing car seat for as

long as possible up to the manufacturer's weight/height limit, then a forward-facing seat with

harness, then a belt-positioning booster until 145 cm (4 ft 9 in) or 8-12 years; gates at both stair ends;

waterproofing — the highest drowning rate occurs in 1- to 2-year-olds, so constant supervision is

needed in and around all water; water heater ≤49°C; poisoning only locked cabinets protect

(cosmetics, analgesics, cleaners top exposures); hot-car hyperthermia deaths are preventable.

Preschooler — 3-6 years

Development: Erikson initiative vs guilt; the superego/conscience develops; magical thinking makes

words literal — say "that was a bad thing to do," not "you are bad"; identification with the same-sex

parent resolves the Oedipal stage.

Fears: the greatest number and variety of fears appear in the preschool years (dark, being left alone,

animals, ghosts, annihilation); most resolve by 5-6 years. Management: night-light,

desensitization/modeling, relaxation, brave self-talk. Refer severe fears that disrupt family life.

Imaginary friends usually appear 2½-3 years and last until school entry (more common in firstborns

and girls) — a sign of health; parents may acknowledge the friend but the child stays responsible for

behavior.

Play: associative play (similar activity, no rigid rules); dramatic/imitative play is central; 4-5-year-olds

reproduce significant-adult behavior.

Language: vocabulary grows from ~300 words at 2 years to >2100 by the end of 5 years.

School readiness: no single readiness indicator; social maturation (especially attention span) matters

as much as academics; school begins between ages 5 and 6.

Nutrition: ~90 kcal/kg ≈ 1800 kcal/day; fluids ~100 ml/kg/day; protein 13-19 g/day; fiber = age + 5

g; saturated fat <10% of calories; calcium 1000 mg/day (4-8 yr); juice/sweetened drinks ≤4-6 oz/day;

follow the 5-2-1-0 framework (5 fruits/vegetables, ≤2 hours of screen, 0 sugar-sweetened drinks, 1

hour activity); preschoolers eat about half an adult portion.

Sleep & dental: ~12 hours nightly with infrequent naps; night waking is common. Primary dentition

is complete by the start of the preschool years; brushing still needs adult assistance and flossing is a

parent's task; continue professional care and fluoride.

Discipline: verbal limit-setting works better than with toddlers — the child obeys danger warnings;

allowing questions and disagreement fosters independence; time-out and solitary play stop

aggression; spanking teaches aggression "as a lesson"; encourage verbal expression of feelings.

Safety: less prone to falls and more aware of danger; poisoning still dangerous (mouthing has

ceased); pedestrian-vehicle injuries increase; teaching replaces pure protection — parents model

behavior (bicycle helmets); electrical-outlet caps can be discontinued with an explanation.

Red flags: severe fears disrupting family life; extreme aggression (needs professional treatment);

stuttering with no improvement over >6 months → speech evaluation; injury-prevention education

for 4½-year-old risk takers.

School-age — 6-12 years

Growth: ~5 cm/yr; weight gain 2-3 kg/yr; average 6-year-old ≈116 cm/21 kg; average 12-year-old

≈150 cm/40 kg; girls surpass boys in height and weight near the end. Prepubescence is the ~2-year

period preceding puberty; first physiologic signs about 9 years (girls). All primary teeth are shed in

this span — the first tooth (central mandibular incisors) is lost at about 6 years ("age of the loose

tooth"); the 6-year molar erupts behind the deciduous molars; 60% of children have dental caries by

age 5.

Psychosocial: Erikson industry vs inferiority — competence through skills; peers become central

(same-sex "best friends", clubs with codes and conformity); bullying affects ~25% of elementary

students as bully or victim, and cyberbullying can be more harmful (anonymous, wide audience);

teach reporting and family conversation. Physical maturity ≠ emotional maturity.

Cognitive: concrete operations 7-11 years; conservation of number 5-6, liquid/mass/length 6-7,

weight 9-10, volume 9-12; reading is the most significant school skill; homework: quiet, well-lit area,

no TV until done, explain rather than answer.

Nutrition: caloric needs are lower than preschool and lower than the adolescent growth spurt;

children eat what the family eats; encourage fruit, nuts, and wholesome snacks; obesity prevalence is

stable at ~17% and higher in rural/socioeconomically distressed families; school-lunch programs

must supply 1/3 of the daily RDA.

Safety: motor-vehicle crashes are the most common cause of severe accidental injury/death (as

passenger or pedestrian); children <13 years ride in the rear, never the front passenger seat with an

Page 11 — air bag; over 18 kg (40 lb) use a forward-facing convertible seat if it fits, then a boost

air bag; over 18 kg (40 lb) use a forward-facing convertible seat if it fits, then a booster until the lap-

shoulder belt fits; no one rides in a pickup-truck bed; ATVs are not recommended under 16 years;

bicycle helmets cut head injury ~45%, brain injury ~33%, facial injury ~27%, fatal injury ~29% —

helmet every ride, the bike must fit; trampolines: single jumper, no somersaults, ≥6 years; skateboards

<5 years never, 6-10 only with close supervision and full padding; participation physical exams ≥every

2 years for sports; firearms locked.

Sleep & dental: sleep falls from ~11.5 hours at 5 years to ~9 hours at 11; up to 50% of children have

nightmares; sleepwalking is common (3-5% frequent) in the first 3-4 hours of sleep. Children may

need help with brushing until ~7-10 years — parents monitor and floss at least daily; brush 2-3×/day

for 2-3 minutes with fluoridated toothpaste; dental examinations every 6 months; dental sealants are

recommended for all school-age children.

Discipline: no corporal punishment — reasoning is effective; time-out for acute behavior; advanced

strategies include withholding privileges, recompense, penalties, and contracting; problem-solving is

the best limit-setting approach; discipline works best in a positive, supportive relationship.

Guidance box highlights: age 6 — ravenous appetite, bicycle safety, privacy (separate bedroom),

peer interaction; ages 7-10 — allergies may appear, minor injuries increase, growth of peer

involvement, independence with limits; ages 11-12 — prepare for pubescent body changes, accurate

sex education, allow regression while supporting growing up.

Stress signals (explore further): stomach pains/headache, sleep problems, bed-wetting, eating-

habit changes, aggressive/stubborn behavior, reluctance to participate, regression (e.g., thumb

sucking).

Adolescent — 12-20 years

The full adolescent assessment (confidentiality, Tanner staging, HEEADSSS interview, risk-behavior

screening, annual visits) is built in Chapter 8. Guidance points that belong to every clinic visit here:

Annual influenza vaccination (live-attenuated or inactivated) is recommended for all adolescents.

Tdap: recommended at 11-18 years for those who completed the childhood DTaP/DTP series and

have not received a tetanus booster; may be given before the 5-year Td interval to close the pertussis

gap.

Meningococcal (MenACWY): at 11-12 years with a booster before age 16; a single dose at 13-18

years if never vaccinated.

HPV: the 9-valent vaccine (Gardasil 9) from a minimum age of 9 years, routine at 11-12 years; two

doses if the series starts at 9-14 years, three doses for a start at 15 years or later or in

immunocompromised persons.

Varicella: for those without a history of infection — two doses ≥4 weeks apart for ages ≥13 years.

Hepatitis B: three-dose series for anyone not previously covered. Hepatitis A in targeted areas or

when immunity is desired.

Confidentiality: offers of confidential services are critical for sensitive topics (sexual behavior,

substance use); many teenagers avoid care if they believe their parents will be told.

HEEADSSS interview (Ch. 8): Home / Education / Eating / Activities / Drugs / Sexuality / Suicide-

depression / Safety.
