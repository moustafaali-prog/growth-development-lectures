Speaker notes 1. Growth and development are continuous processes Meaning: From conception until death, human beings go through continuous change: cells divide, organs grow, abilities develop, functions mature. There are no “gaps” in development; even when change seems slow or hidden, internal processes keep going. Importance in nursing: There is no age at which growth or development stops; even adolescents or adults continue to change (e.g. psychosocially, cognitively). So in pediatric nursing, knowing that earlier periods set the stage for later ones is essential. 2. Each child has his/her own rate of growth and development Meaning: Although there are typical milestones and “norms,” children differ in how fast or slow they reach them. Genetic inheritance, nutrition, health status, environment, cultural context all affect the rate. Example: Two 3-year-olds: one might speak full sentences, another still combining two words. Both might be “normal” if within the accepted range. Why it matters: Nurses must avoid comparing children harshly; must use range of expected ages, look for red flags rather than exact match. Be alert to delays, but respect individual variation. 3. Growth and development are synchronous, yet different parts/domains develop at different rates Meaning: “Synchronous” here means that growth (physical size, bodily parts) and development (functional / behavioral / cognitive changes) occur together, but the pace by which different parts or domains change is not uniform. Each body part (or system) has its own timetable. Some parts mature early, others later. Some skills emerge earlier than others. Example: Infants gain head control before trunk control; infants may hear early but speech comes later; emotional / social skills may lag behind physical/motor skills. Nursing implication: Assessment must cover multiple domains (physical, motor, cognitive, emotional). A delay in one domain doesn’t always mean global delay but needs evaluation. 4. All domains of growth & development are interrelated (physical, psychosocial, cognitive, motor) to meet the child’s basic physiological needs Meaning: The child does not grow in only one area; the physical growth, motor abilities, cognitive readiness, social / emotional maturity interact. Physical growth permits motor skills; motor skills enable exploration; exploration fosters cognitive learning, which supports social skills, and so on. Also, basic physiological needs (nutrition, sleep, health) enable all domains. Example: A child who is undernourished may have stunted physical growth, which affects motor skills (weak muscles), which in turn restricts ability to explore, leading to less environmental stimulation, possibly slower cognitive / social development. Clinical relevance: Interventions that ignore one domain (e.g. cognitive) may fail if another domain (like nutrition / physical health) is impaired. 5. Children’s behavior is directed toward competence Meaning: Children naturally strive to master tasks, gain new skills, become more independent. Behavior is often goal-oriented: to do things themselves, to assert autonomy, to explore, learn. Example: A toddler insisting on feeding him/herself, wanting to dress, wanting to help, even if not perfectly. This drive to competence is part of psychosocial development (e.g. autonomy vs shame in Erikson). Nursing use: Support and encourage age-appropriate autonomy. Offer choices, allow safe trial and error, foster self-care. 6. Growth & development progress from the midline to periphery (proximal → distal) Meaning: Also called the proximodistal principle. Development begins in structures nearest the center of the body (torso, spine) and proceeds outward toward the limbs, hands, fingers. Thus, control over the torso develops before control over arms and legs; arms before hands; hands before fingers. Example: Infants can move shoulders or arms before they can control fingers to pick up tiny objects (pincer grasp). 7. Growth & development occur in an orderly (sequential) sequence Meaning: Skills and capacities develop in a logical order: one skill provides the foundation for the next. Usually simpler / general functions develop before more complex / specific ones. You can predict patterns, though timing may vary. Example: Infants roll → sit → crawl → stand → walk. In cognitive / language: crying → cooing → babbling → single words → short sentences. 8. The direction of development is cephalocaudal (head to toe) Meaning: Earlier and more rapid development occurs in the head region; infants gain control of head and neck first, then trunk, then lower limbs. Control over head → ability to support, hold up → then control trunk, then legs. Example: Before an infant can walk, he/she must be able to hold up head, then sit, then crawl. 9. General → Specific (from global to differentiated) Meaning: Early responses are generalized; later they become more specialized and precise. Early perception / behavior is vague; gradually discrimination occurs. Early motor movements are gross; later fine motor skills develop. Early recognition is broad; later specific. Example: An infant may initially respond to many faces similarly; over time begins to discriminate mother’s face, then facial expressions. Or, early hand movement is a full wave; later becomes precise finger manipulation. 10. Development is relative Meaning: Growth / development must be considered relative to contexts: cultural norms, individual baseline, expectations, environmental support. What is “normal” in one culture or setting may differ. Also, relative means that growth & development can be influenced (accelerated or delayed) by factors like illness, environment, stimulation, nutrition.

Speaker notes Plot on Percentile Charts: You plot these measurements on percentile charts, which are graphs that display a range of values from the lowest (0th percentile) to the highest (100th percentile). Each percentile line represents a specific percentage of the population. For example, the 50th percentile represents the median or middle value for the general population. Comparison: By plotting a child's measurements on these charts, you can see where their measurements fall in relation to the general population. For instance, if a child's height measurement is on the 75th percentile line, it means that their height is greater than or equal to 75% of children in the reference population. Interpretation: The interpretation of these percentile measurements depends on the specific parameter being assessed. For example, if a child consistently falls below the 5th percentile for weight, it might indicate underweight or slow growth, while measurements consistently above the 95th percentile might indicate overweight or rapid growth. However, it's essential to consider various factors, including genetics and individual growth patterns, when interpreting percentile data.

Growth Charts

Speaker notes Plot on Percentile Charts: You plot these measurements on percentile charts, which are graphs that display a range of values from the lowest (0th percentile) to the highest (100th percentile). Each percentile line represents a specific percentage of the population. For example, the 50th percentile represents the median or middle value for the general population. Comparison: By plotting a child's measurements on these charts, you can see where their measurements fall in relation to the general population. For instance, if a child's height measurement is on the 75th percentile line, it means that their height is greater than or equal to 75% of children in the reference population. Interpretation: The interpretation of these percentile measurements depends on the specific parameter being assessed. For example, if a child consistently falls below the 5th percentile for weight, it might indicate underweight or slow growth, while measurements consistently above the 95th percentile might indicate overweight or rapid growth. However, it's essential to consider various factors, including genetics and individual growth patterns, when interpreting percentile data.

Understanding Growth Percentiles

Percentile Definition: A statistical value indicating the percentage of reference children who fall at or below a specific measurement.

50th Percentile: Represents the exact median parameter for age and sex. Half of healthy peers are larger, half are smaller.

Normal Range: Generally defined between the 5th and 95th percentiles on standardized WHO/CDC curves.

Trend vs. Static Value: A single percentile reading is merely a snapshot. Serial measurements over time (the growth curve) are clinically vital.

Clinical Interpretation Example

Key Rule from Wong's Textbook:

"Percentiles are not grades in school. Being in the 10th percentile is not 'worse' than being in the 90th percentile. Consistency along an established trajectory is the primary indicator of normal physical growth."

If a 12-month-old boy is in the 10th percentile for length:• He is taller than 10% of 12-month-old boys.• 90% of peers are taller than him.• Clinical Assessment: HEALTHY if he has consistently tracked along the 10th curve since birth.

4.1 Site Profile — Role of the Well-Child Clinic

The well-child clinic is where positive health is promoted in normal, healthy children. Visits focus

primarily on anticipatory guidance — teaching what to expect next and how to prevent harm

before the child reaches the susceptible age — ideally beginning during pregnancy.

Goal: periodic screening and the prevention of disease. The AAP recommends periodic check-ups for

all infants and children.

Nurse's role: carry out the recommended periodic screenings and the current immunization

schedule; educate the family at every visit.

Anticipatory guidance: one third of all childhood injuries occur in the home — most are predictable

and preventable when teaching precedes the susceptible age.

Cultural respect: explore the family's cultural practices around growth and development; most are

harmless and can be supported. Never assume practices from skin color, accent, or name.

School-age — 6-12 years

Growth: ~5 cm/yr; weight gain 2-3 kg/yr; average 6-year-old ≈116 cm/21 kg; average 12-year-old

teach reporting and family conversation. Physical maturity ≠ emotional maturity.

Cognitive: concrete operations 7-11 years; conservation of number 5-6, liquid/mass/length 6-7,

weight 9-10, volume 9-12; reading is the most significant school skill; homework: quiet, well-lit area,

5.1 Site Profile — Role of the Nursery

Many mothers work outside the home, there are many single-parent families, and many families

live far from relatives — in all of these circumstances infants may need to be cared for outside

the home, in child care settings or home day care centers. The nursery rotation is where the

nurse observes and supports that daily care.

Types: sitter in the home, traditional (freestanding) day care center, or home-based day care center

with fewer children.

Licensing: families using a freestanding or home-based center should make sure the provider is

appropriately licensed.

Caregiver-to-child ratio: parents should feel comfortable with the ratio; the number of caregivers

per child follows the licensed facility's staffing plan.

Staff training: caregivers should be trained in infant CPR and first aid — and parents should be

taught first-aid measures and infant/child CPR as well, to minimize the consequences of injury.

Separation anxiety: it is helpful to visit the center once or twice beforehand so the infant gets used

to the caregivers from the comfort of the parent's lap. Warn parents that separation anxiety in late

infancy can cause a disturbing crying episode when the parent leaves — and reassure them the infant

will not suffer harm from the separation.

RULE OF THUMB FOR RASHES AND FEVER

A fever (temperature above the normal range) together with a new generalised rash, lethargy, or

poor feeding is a call-the-provider situation. Teething does not cause high fever, vomiting, or

diarrhea — a temperature elevation with those signs means illness, not teeth. Exclusion criteria (for

5.4 Daily Care Procedures

Feeding by stage (0-3 years)

Infant fluid & calories: fluid 140-160 mL/kg/day (newborn); ~100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg plus

50 mL/kg/day for the next 10 kg; calories 105-108 kcal/kg/day (1-6 months: 108 kcal/kg; 6-12

months: 98 kcal/kg).

Breastfeeding at the center: expressed breast milk fed by bottle — never microwaved; feed on the

baby's cues; the properly latched infant sucks rhythmically with audible swallowing.

Formula: prepare exactly per package instructions; 2-3 oz per feeding about 6×/day as a starter

guide; never prop the bottle and never put juice or sweetened liquid in a bottle.

Solids: begin with a single-ingredient iron-fortified cereal, then fruits and vegetables; introduce one

new food at a time and watch for reactions (Ch. 4 §4.4 infant).

Toddler meals: 3 meals + 2 snacks, small portions; milk 2-3 servings (24-28 oz/day equivalent); juice

capped at 4-6 oz/day of 100% juice in a cup.

Choking prevention while feeding: slowly add more difficult-to-chew foods as the toddler becomes

adept; cut all foods into bite-size pieces; avoid nuts, gumdrops/chewy candies, raw carrots, peanut

butter alone, and popcorn; cut hot dogs and grapes into quarters; cook carrots until soft or grate

them; always supervise the toddler while eating.

Self-feeding: child-sized spoon and fork with dull tines; seat the toddler securely with feet

supported, not dangling; never leave the toddler unattended while eating; minimize distractions (turn

off the TV).

Hygiene, bathing & diapering

Bathing safety: infants can drown in a very small amount of water — never leave an infant

unattended in a sink, baby bathtub, standard bathtub, swimming or wading pool, or any body of

water even if shallow; keep the bathroom door closed and the toilet lid down; empty tubs, pails, and

buckets immediately after use.

5Page 5

Diapering: changing diapers is part of the infant's daily care; the wet-diaper count (about six per 24

hours) is the caregiver's daily hydration check. Follow the center's universal-hygiene procedure at

every change (gloves, cleaning, hand hygiene, safe disposal).

Hand hygiene: handwashing is part of infant-care practice; apply standard infection-control hand

hygiene before and after every child contact, meal, and diaper change.

Safe sleep & teething

Safe sleep: place infants on the back for sleep (SIDS risk reduction); cribs must not have pillows,

comforters, stuffed animals, or other soft items; keep plastic bags of any size away from infants; keep

window-blind and drapery cords out of the infant's reach.

Teething: first lower central incisors appear ~6-10 months (average 8 months); cold items are

soothing; fever, vomiting, or diarrhea are not teething signs — treat them as illness.

Play & stimulation

Infants: toys recommended for 0-12 months only; avoid stuffed animals with eyes or buttons that

can be dislodged; keep the floor free of small dropped items.

Toddlers: parallel play (alongside, not with, others) with imitation — the best "toy" is adult-child

interaction.

Reading, storytelling, and interactive adult-child communication positively affect language

development.

6.1 Site Profile — Role of the Kindergarten

Preschool should be used primarily as an opportunity to foster the child's social skills and

6.3 Vision & Hearing Screening

VISION

Indication: the USPSTF recommends vision screening for the presence of amblyopia and its risk

factors for all children 3-5 years.

Alignment tests: corneal light reflex (Hirschberg) from about 40.5 cm (16 in) — light symmetrical in

normal eyes, off-center in misalignment; epicanthal folds can mimic misalignment

(pseudostrabismus, common in Asian children). Cover test at near (33 cm / 13 in) and far (6 m / 20

ft); alternate cover test.

Strabismus window: if misalignment is not detected and corrected by ages 4-6 years, blindness

from disuse (amblyopia) may result.

Acuity: the tumbling E and HOTV are excellent tests for preschool-age children; other options:

Snellen letters/numbers, picture test, Allen figures, LEA symbols.

Referral criterion: fewer than 4 of 6 correct on the 20-foot (6-m) line.

Peripheral vision (cooperative child): normal ~50° upward, 70° downward, 60° nasalward, 90°

temporally.

HEARING

Recommendation: the AAP recommends pure-tone audiometry screening at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 kHz;

a child fails if the tones cannot be heard at 20 dB HL.

Age-appropriate methods: behavioral audiometry for toddlers; play audiometry for the toddler;

pure-tone audiometry for children and adolescents; tympanometry (middle-ear function) at all ages;

EOAE for cochlear (outer hair cell) response.

Suspicion: maintain a high index of suspicion for children with conditions associated with hearing

loss, whose parents report concern, or whose behavior suggests auditory impairment.

6.5 Play, Behavior & Socialization

Associative play: preschoolers especially enjoy associative play — group play in similar or identical

activities; the parallel play of the toddler (alongside, not with others) gives way to group activity.

Imagination: activity is imitative, imaginative, and dramatic; dress-up is central; imaginary friends

appear 2½-3 years and last until school entry; most fears resolve by 5-6 years.

6Page 6

Sharing & friendships: by kindergarten entry the child cooperates, shares (things and feelings),

shows kindness/generosity/affection, holds conversation, expresses feelings, helps others, and makes

friends.

Separation: during the preschool period the separation-individuation phase matures; preschoolers

tolerate brief separations with little or no protest — the nurse normalizes this for worried parents.

Discipline: the preschooler responds to consistent limit-setting with simple, concrete explanations —

words are taken literally (magical thinking), so the parent says "that was a bad thing to do," not "you

are bad".

8.2 Physical Growth During Puberty

The final 20% to 25% of linear growth is achieved during puberty, and up to 50% of ideal adult

body weight is gained during this time.

Girls begin puberty and reach maturity about 1½ to 2 years earlier than boys; the pubertal growth

spurt begins as early as 9½ or as late as 14½ years in girls, and as early as 10½ and as late as 16

years in boys.

The growth spurt reaches its peak at about 12 years in girls and about 14 years in boys.

Peak height velocity (PHV) occurs at about 12 years of age in girls, around 6 to 12 months before

menarche; PHV is used as a predictor of menarche, and height at menarche predicts ultimate adult

height. Few girls grow more than 5 cm (2 inches) after menarche; growth in girls' height usually

ceases 2 to 2½ years after menarche.

Boys typically reach PHV at about 14 years, after growth of the testicles and penis and appearance of

axillary and mature pubic hair; growth in height ceases at 18 or 20 years.

Peak weight velocity occurs about 6 months after PHV in girls (and about 6 months after menarche);

in boys the weight and height spurts occur simultaneously.

Leg-length increase precedes trunk growth by about 6 to 9 months and shoulders/chest by about 1

year: "outgrow shoes first, then pants, then shirts".

Body composition: girls' fat mass increases markedly in early puberty and continues to increase after

menarche; boys have a peak deceleration in fat-mass accumulation at the growth spurt, with

significantly higher lean body mass overall.

On average, girls gain 5-20 cm (2-8 inches) in height and 7-25 kg (15.5-55 lb) in weight during

adolescence. In the U.S., onset of Tanner stage 2 genital development in boys (mean): non-Hispanic

white 10.14 yr, African-American 9.14 yr, Hispanic 10.04 yr.

8.3 Sexual Maturation — Tanner Staging

Tanner stages describe pubertal development in numbered stages; in females they describe

development based on breast size and the shape and distribution of pubic hair, in males on

genital development and pubic hair; stage 1 = prepubertal, stage 5 = adult.

Example entries (boys): Stage 1 (prepubertal): no pubic hair — essentially the same as during

childhood, no distinction between hair on the pubis and over the abdomen. Stage 4: increased size of

the penis with growth in diameter and development of the glans; glans larger and broader; scrotum

darker; pubic hair more abundant with curling.

Fertility: from a clinical perspective an adolescent should be considered potentially fertile with a first

menstrual period or a first ejaculation; viable sperm production tends to follow the first ejaculation by

about 1 year.

Boys do not experience a discrete event analogous to menstruation; the capacity to ejaculate appears

approximately 1 year after initial testicular enlargement and the appearance of pubic hair.

8.5 Body Image & Eating Disorders

Body image is important for adolescent girls; a common behaviour is extensive dieting at a time

when physical changes are occurring, and a focus on slimness and dieting may trigger the

development of eating disorders.

Anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa occur among adolescents; screen with non-judgmental

questions about meal patterns, dieting behaviour, and weight concerns.

Body image and gender-role definition are nearly secured by late adolescence; identity consolidates.

Acne and feelings of sadness or depression are among the health concerns adolescents report.

This guide covers the detection and referral signals for eating disorders; diagnosis and treatment

follow specialist protocols.

8.6 Injury Prevention & Safety

Injuries kill more U.S. adolescents than any other single cause; unintentional injury accounted for

48% of deaths among 12-19-year-olds (1996-2005), and its share rose with age.

Motor-vehicle crashes are the single greatest source of unintentional injury and death in young

people; contributory factors: lacking driving experience and maturity, following too closely, speeding,

other teen passengers in the car, alcohol use.

Homicide is the second leading cause of death among all U.S. adolescents and the most likely cause

for African-American teenagers; in 2007, 82% of homicides among 13-19-year-olds were firearm-

related.

In 2011, 5.1% of adolescents nationwide reported carrying a weapon on one or more days in the past

month (down from 27% in 1991); teens targeted for repeated teasing/harassment are more likely to

skip school, report depression symptoms, attempt suicide, and bring weapons to school.

Sports: participation physical examinations at least every 2 years; teach and model helmet use for

cycling, skating, and helmeted sports (a helmet reduces head injury ~45% and brain injury ~33%).

Cover injury-prevention counseling at every visit per the Bright Futures "violence and injury

prevention" item.

8.8 Psychosocial Assessment & Health Education

INTERVIEW DOMAINS

School: recent grades, absences, suspensions, repeated grades; school-related strengths and

deficits; involvement in activities.

Peers: quality of peer relationships — may identify socially isolated teens.

Future plans: education/employment and career choices — a window into sources of stress.

Strengths: teach coping-with-stress skills and involvement in personally meaningful activities to

support emotional health.

Emotional well-being: sadness, depression, suicidal ideation; adolescents whose personal fable

stresses their uniqueness are at higher risk for depression and suicidal ideation, while a strong sense

of invulnerability predicts risky behaviour.

Risk behaviours: below-average grades correlate with tobacco/alcohol use, unprotected

intercourse, and suicide attempts; violence and harassment on the basis of race, gender, or sexual

orientation affect more than half of students.

These domains are commonly memorised with the HEEADSSS mnemonic used in adolescent teaching

materials — Home, Education, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide-depression, Safety — and every

question it prompts maps to the content here.
